\title{Swapping Initial Motor Temperatures Changes Route Feasibility for a Simulated Hexapod}
\begin{abstract}
We tested whether swapping which motors start warm changes which routes a hexapod can finish when its average and maximum starting temperatures are held fixed. In MuJoCo, the hexapod traversed four routes on each of 12 two-stage courses at three thermal settings. We executed all routes under both warm-side assignments, giving 288 continuous walks across 72 starts. In 11 of 36 course--setting pairs, each assignment had at least one finishable route and no route finished under both assignments. This occurred on eight courses. We compared a planner that received the motor temperatures individually with a planner that received their mean and maximum and averaged forecasts over the two possible assignments. Both used the same temperature-responsive speed controller with individual temperatures during walking. The per-motor planner finished 26 of 72 starts with two thermal stops. The aggregate planner finished seven with five stops. The gain was 26.4 percentage points, with a descriptive 95\% course-resampling interval of 15.3 to 40.3 points. The exhaustive outcomes and the matched planner comparison show how the initial distribution of heat across motors can change route feasibility and completion under the tested simulation conditions.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
Actuator temperature, hexapod robots, legged locomotion, path planning, thermal management
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}

\PARstart{W}{hen} a robot starts with several warm motors, those motors have less thermal headroom. Routes can load different legs, especially on banks that slope in opposite directions. Two starting states may have identical temperature values, with the warm values assigned to different legs. We ask whether that change alone can change which complete routes reach the goal without overheating.

We test this question in simulation by assigning the same nine warm and nine cool temperatures to opposite sides of a hexapod and replaying every route under both assignments. The robot walks continuously through a junction and adjusts its speed as a function of its motor temperatures using an adaptation of Trujillo and Cutkosky's controller \cite{trujillo2009}. The route replays establish which routes finish in each starting state, including routes that the planners reject. A separate matched comparison tests the value of providing the full temperature assignment when choosing the route. Both arms share the execution controller and differ in the initial temperature information supplied for route selection.

The primary result measures how often both starting states are finishable and require different routes. The planner comparison measures differences in on-time completion and thermal stops across the same starts. An earlier matched experiment tested whether planning a cooling pause at a junction improved completion. We present that experiment separately in Appendix~\ref{app:pause}.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Thermal Modeling And Control}

Lumped thermal models predict temperature transients in electrical machines \cite{mellor1991}. De et al. incorporated both motor thermal dynamics and the timing of a representative task in legged-robot actuator sizing \cite{de2011}. Lin et al. identified a coupled thermal network for a quadruped that incorporates heat exchange between components and forced convection during locomotion \cite{lin2025}. In our simulation, each actuator's temperature evolves independently according to its applied torque history and the cooling term.

Trujillo and Cutkosky regulated climbing speed using a motor thermal constraint \cite{trujillo2009}. Tan et al. included temperature limits in robot trajectory optimization, and Kawaharazuka et al. estimated and controlled motor core temperature while updating model parameters online \cite{tan2018heat,kawaharazuka2020}. Temperature constraints have also been imposed on motor drivers in predictive control for a redundant manipulator \cite{debenedittis2026}. Two recent quadruped preprints address thermal limits at the locomotion-policy level. Wan et al. conditioned a residual policy on motor thermal state, and Ségas et al. trained a policy to reduce the highest predicted steady-state motor temperatures \cite{wan2026,segas2026}.

Faigl and \v{C}\'{\i}\v{z}ek measured servo temperatures during uneven-terrain trials with a hexapod and used standing breaks for cooling \cite{faigl2019}. Brodoline et al. measured power use in a static pose on a servomotor leg test bench \cite{brodoline2024}. Jorgensen et al. optimized contact-constrained postures to reduce predicted actuator temperatures and demonstrated the approach on Valkyrie \cite{jorgensen2019}. Urata and Ito described predicting individual motor temperatures during continued walking and during a transition to a standing cooling posture \cite{urata2017}.

\subsection{Terrain, Contact Loads, And Energy}

Hexapod studies have used joint torque and inertial measurements to adapt compliance to terrain, and optimized contact-force distribution during walking on rugged terrain \cite{zhao2021,ding2024}. For quadrupeds on slopes, Gehring et al. adapted locomotion to estimated inclination, and Focchi et al. optimized contact forces to limit actuator effort while satisfying friction constraints \cite{gehring2015,focchi2017}. Righetti et al. showed how contact-force objectives can be incorporated into floating-base robot control \cite{righetti2013}. These studies motivate tracking actuator effort along each branch in the simulation.

Energy studies of hexapod locomotion have examined swing trajectories on irregular ground and selection among gaits by energy use and cost of transport \cite{luneckas2019,luneckas2021}. Seok et al. accounted for motor heat loss when characterizing energy use in the MIT Cheetah \cite{seok2015}. Schperberg et al. optimized step placement and timing parameters for efficiency and robustness \cite{schperberg2025}. Our route screen uses predicted peak motor temperature and arrival time.

\subsection{Terrain-Aware Locomotion Planning}

Contact planning on varied terrain has combined discrete search over stance or contact sequences with continuous motion planning \cite{hauser2008,valouch2021}. Jenelten et al. optimized footholds from onboard elevation maps, while Mastalli et al. jointly planned body motion, footholds, and step durations with terrain costs \cite{jenelten2020,mastalli2020}. Brandão et al. optimized a mechanical work model for humanoid footsteps subject to feasibility and ground friction constraints on slanted and slippery terrain \cite{brandao2016}.

At route scale, Prágr et al. learned gait-specific traversability models for hexapod exploration and used them to choose navigation waypoints \cite{pragr2022}. Fu et al. coupled global path planning with proprioceptive information from the walking robot, and Yang et al. planned routes for a quadruped using learned motion costs that included energy, time, and risk \cite{fu2022,yang2021}. David et al. adjusted navigation costs using the robot's internal hazard state under environmental heat and radiation exposure \cite{david2022}. Our study fixes the initial temperature values and changes their assignment to motors. It exhaustively checks which routes can finish under each assignment while the speed controller responds to temperature.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Course Network And Execution Outcomes}
\label{subsec:routing}

Each course has a start, a flat junction, and a goal. Two branches connect the start to the junction and two connect the junction to the goal. Each of the four routes is specified by its branch number in the first stage and its branch number in the second stage. Each replay follows the specified route in one continuous run. The chassis, foot, controller, and motor states carry through the junction.

\caption{Top view of persistent-bank course P3. Each stage has two branch centerlines. The centerlines meet at the flat junction. The four routes are the combinations of the two branch choices in each stage.}
\label{fig:graph}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:graph} shows one of the 12 synthetic heightfields. Smooth approach and exit curves connect the common landings to each branch. The bank fades in and out along the branch and tapers laterally. The courses vary the approach and exit lengths, bank lengths, lateral offsets, bank angles, and longitudinal bank shifts. There are four alternating-bank courses (A1--A4) whose signs reverse across stages on each branch, four persistent-bank courses (P1--P4) whose signs persist, and four courses (S1--S4) with a stage in which both branches bank in the same direction. Geometry and deadlines were fixed before the nominal profiles and warm replays. Appendix~\ref{app:course-data} lists the geometry.

The target heightfield grid spacing was 0.12 m. The projected forward lengths were 38.03--39.56 m. The deadline for each course was its projected length divided by 0.108 m/s and rounded to a whole second, giving 352--366 s. The rate preserved the earlier benchmark's deadline scale. Both warm-side assignments and both planners used the same deadlines. Arrival required the chassis to enter the 0.55 m radius around the goal. We checked the lateral error from the route centerline at the same forward coordinate and used a limit of 0.85 m. The initial stand counts toward the deadline.

A completion required arrival within the deadline without a fall, chassis contact, corridor departure, or thermal stop. The simulator stops a run at the first recorded sample at which any motor reaches $T_{\mathrm{lim}}=70\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$. It checks this condition before checking arrival in the same sample. The planners use a forecast cutoff of $T_{\mathrm{cap}}=T_{\mathrm{lim}}-0.50\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

\subsection{Hexapod And Physics Execution}

The simulation used MuJoCo 3.9.0 \cite{todorov2012}. The hexapod had a floating chassis and three torque-actuated joints per leg at the coxa, femur, and tibia. The simplified geometry was informed by the computer-aided design (CAD) assembly and had a modeled total mass of 82 kg, including the feet. From proximal to distal, the segment lengths were 0.157, 0.550, and 0.740 m, and the nominal joint torque limits were 90, 150, and 130 $\mathrm{N}{\cdot}\mathrm{m}$. A 3 s crawl gait generated foot targets from the branch centerline and simulated terrain height. Joint position and velocity feedback generated tracking torque. For supporting feet, a regularized least-squares allocation distributed the desired chassis force and moment. The physics step was 2 ms. The simulator recorded body position and contact counts and updated the thermal model every 20 ms using the root-mean-square applied actuator torque from that interval. Each walk began with a 2 s stand.

The 3 s gait staggers foot phases by 0.50 s. Each foot swings for 13.5\% of a cycle with a 0.09 m lift at mid-swing. The controller sets touchdown from the centerline pose and terrain height ahead of the body. When at least three feet meet the stance-support test, the force allocator distributes the commanded six-dimensional body wrench across them. For its unconstrained step, with support matrix $\mathbf{A}$ and body wrench $\mathbf{w}$, the allocated forces are
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}
   \left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}
    +0.02\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{w}.
\end{equation}
The controller limits vertical forces to nonnegative values and horizontal magnitudes to at most 0.65 times the vertical force. The foot Jacobians map ground-reaction forces to joint torque terms. The resulting torques, combined with joint feedback, are clipped at the motor torque limits before stepping physics.

\subsection{Thermal Dynamics And Warm Starts}

McMordie et al. estimated actuator torque from motor speed, battery voltage, and drive command on RHex, and Hwangbo et al. fitted actuator dynamics from measured joint signals on ANYmal \cite{mcmordie2003,hwangbo2019}. The present thermal calculation uses the applied actuator torque recorded in simulation.

The simulator tracks one temperature for each motor and updates the 18 states independently. For a 20 ms sample containing $n=10$ physics steps, the actuator root-mean-square torque is
\begin{equation}
q_i=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n M_{ij}^2\right)^{1/2},
\end{equation}
where $M_{ij}$ is the applied actuator torque at physics step $j$. The thermal model uses the equilibrium $T_{i,\mathrm{eq}}$ for squared torque $q_i^2$ and updates the temperature over each interval $\Delta t$.

\begin{equation}
T_{i,\mathrm{eq}} = T_{\mathrm{amb}} + R\left(\frac{q_i}{q_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^2 ,
\label{eq:equilibrium}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
T_i(t+\Delta t) = T_{i,\mathrm{eq}} +
\bigl[T_i(t)-T_{i,\mathrm{eq}}\bigr]\exp(-\Delta t/\tau).
\label{eq:thermal-update}
\end{equation}

Ambient temperature $T_{\mathrm{amb}}$ was 25 \ensuremath{{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}} and reference torque $q_{\mathrm{ref}}$ was 55 N\ensuremath{\cdot}m. The main experiment and the matched pause experiment used the settings in Table~\ref{tab:settings}. All had a cooling time constant $\tau$ of 120 s. The parameter $R$ is the modeled equilibrium rise above ambient at the reference torque. For relative heating multiplier $m$, the configured settings give $R=72m\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

\subsection{Branch Profiles And Route Forecast}

We profiled each branch offline in separate nominal physics simulations at 65\% and 100\% of the 0.12 m/s nominal speed, with thermal derating disabled and a 245 s limit per nominal walk. We grouped samples after the stand by forward coordinate into equal-width bins of width $\Delta x$ close to 0.30 m. For a bin populated by $n_b$ samples, the forward-rate estimate is $\Delta x/(0.02 n_b)$ in meters per second. Motor load is the mean of the $n_b$ squared RMS torque samples. The forecast updates temperatures first with the stand samples from the first branch's nominal-speed profile. The final bin also supplies the short flat approach from the nominal profile's stop point to the intermediate junction.

For a speed command $u$ expressed as a fraction of nominal speed, the forecast uses $\alpha=(u-0.65)/0.35$ to interpolate the forward rate $v_b$ and squared motor loads $\mathbf{L}_b$ in the current branch bin.
\begin{align}
 v_b(\alpha)&=(1-\alpha)v_{b,\mathrm{slow}}+\alpha v_{b,\mathrm{fast}},\\
 \mathbf{L}_b(\alpha)&=(1-\alpha)\mathbf{L}_{b,\mathrm{slow}}
                        +\alpha\mathbf{L}_{b,\mathrm{fast}} .
\end{align}
The forecast advances forward progress and applies Eq.~\eqref{eq:thermal-update} at 0.50 s intervals, using the interpolated squared loads and the speed selected by the governor. Its forward goal approximation is $x_G-0.55$ m. The last forecast increment is clipped to that point. Nominal profiles use unmodified torque limits. Warm replays apply temperature-dependent torque clipping and carry the locomotion state across the junction.

\subsection{Adapted Thermal Speed Controller}

Trujillo and Cutkosky \cite[Sec.~III-D, Eq.~(8)]{trujillo2009} used measured motor case temperature and a winding temperature limit to bound average heating over the upcoming gait period. At each footfall they inverted an empirical heating-versus-speed curve for the four-legged RiSE climber. We apply the fastest-feasible-speed rule to the 18 simulated motors using the first-order model in Eq.~\eqref{eq:thermal-update} and the hexapod's position-dependent branch profiles.

The adapted controller uses a 3 s gait period $g$. For a motor at its current temperature $T_i$, the controller uses the squared-torque budget $B_i$ obtained from Eq.~\eqref{eq:thermal-update} at $T_i(t+g)=T_{\mathrm{cap}}$.
\begin{equation}
B_i = \frac{q_{\mathrm{ref}}^2}{R}
    \left[T_i-T_{\mathrm{amb}}
      +\frac{T_{\mathrm{cap}}-T_i}{1-\exp(-g/\tau)}\right].
\label{eq:squared-torque-budget}
\end{equation}
At 0.50 s intervals during walking, the regulator selects the largest $\alpha\in[0,1]$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
L_{b,i,\mathrm{slow}}+\alpha
  (L_{b,i,\mathrm{fast}}-L_{b,i,\mathrm{slow}})\leq B_i
  \quad\text{for all }i .
\label{eq:speed-constraints}
\end{equation}
If the intersection of all motor bounds is empty, the implementation records an infeasible update and commands the 65\% endpoint. The common torque derating and 70 \ensuremath{{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}} stop remain active during physics execution. Both arms use this controller in forecasting and physics execution.

\subsection{Matched Warm-Side Assignments And Route Selection}

Each combination of course and thermal setting gave a pair of starts. One start placed the nine warm temperatures on the left and nine cool temperatures on the right. The other interchanged the two sides. Thus the initial multiset, mean, and maximum were identical within each pair. The 12 courses and three settings yielded 36 pairs and 72 starts. The four routes were evaluated without a pause in the main experiment.

For each route, the per-motor planner uses the forecast from the assigned starting vector. The aggregate planner receives the mean $\bar T_0$ and maximum $T_{0,\max}$ and knows the two assignments in the test design. It recovers $T_{\mathrm{warm}}=T_{0,\max}$ and $T_{\mathrm{cool}}=2\bar T_0-T_{0,\max}$, then forecasts both possible side assignments. If $P_\ell,P_r$ are the highest temperatures predicted over the respective walks and $D_\ell,D_r$ are the predicted arrival times, the aggregate route scores are $(P_\ell+P_r)/2$ and $(D_\ell+D_r)/2$. Each peak is taken before averaging, and each forecast runs its own simulated speed control. The aggregate planner uses the same scores and makes the same selection for both starts of a pair.

Each planner admits a route when its peak score is strictly below $T_{\mathrm{cap}}$ and its arrival score is at or before the deadline. Among admitted routes, it chooses the earliest arrival score, then lower peak score, then route identifier. If all routes fail the screen, the planner records a rejection. During execution the speed governor receives the evolving individual motor temperatures in both arms. The difference in information is at the route selection stage. The course, starting state, and route determine a common replay outcome for either planner that selects that route.

\subsection{Exhaustive Physics And Evaluation Criteria}

For each warm start we replayed every route whose branches had complete nominal profiles at both speeds. Forecast rejection did not prevent an exhaustive replay. We fixed forecasts and planner choices before running warm physics. Each of the two route-feasibility sets for a pair contains the routes that completed under its warm-side assignment. A necessary route change occurs when both sets are nonempty and disjoint. We also count pairs in which the only finishable route switches, and pairs with a thermal crossover. A thermal crossover requires two distinct routes, one that finishes left-warm and stops thermally right-warm, and one with the opposite outcomes. The pair can still have a third route that finishes under both assignments.

Planner outcomes use all 72 starts per arm as the denominator, including rejections. The practical criterion fixed before warm execution was a completion gain of at least 10 percentage points with no additional thermal stops. We computed a descriptive 95\% interval from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 20,000 resamples of the 12 courses. Each sampled course retains its three thermal settings and both assignments. The interval describes variation across these courses.

We verified each replay by checking the initial temperature vector, sample count, terminal chassis position and outcome, and the first threshold crossing for thermal stops. We recomputed the 18 temperature traces from recorded torques and checked junction event order. The exhaustive outcomes provide two reference counts. One chooses the best route separately for each assignment with replay outcomes known. The other chooses at most one shared route for each pair with outcomes known.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Physical Route Feasibility}

All 96 nominal profiles completed, providing four routes for every start. We evaluated 288 assignment-specific forecasts and pooled them into 144 aggregate route scores. We then executed all 288 warm walks. Of these, 51 finished and 237 stopped at the thermal threshold. There were 162 thermal stops before the junction and 75 after the junction. Every forecast met the deadline, with at least 33.79 s to spare, and no warm replay ended at the deadline.

In 11 of 36 course--setting pairs, both warm-side assignments had a finishable route and the two route sets were disjoint (Table~\ref{tab:classes}). The 11 pairs occurred on eight courses. Six of the 11 pairs had exactly one finishable route in each assignment. All 11 had a thermal crossover. Across all pairs, 13 had a thermal crossover on ten courses. The two remaining crossover pairs each had a route that finished in both assignments. Four pairs had a shared finishable route, five had a finishable route for only one assignment, and 16 had no finishable route under either assignment. Appendix~\ref{app:course-data} lists the full set of finishable routes for each course and setting.

The alternative counts describe the effect of changing the route screen with the walking controller held fixed.

\section{Discussion}

The feasibility result follows from executing the same routes under both assignments. In the 11 disjoint pairs, a route rule that must choose one route for the two indistinguishable summaries can finish at most one start. The replay outcomes quantify that restriction independently of the forecast or its cutoff. The aggregate planner's outcome also reflects its equal-weight scoring rule and the chosen forecast cutoff. Its comparison measures the benefit of individual temperatures for these two planners.

Equation~\eqref{eq:thermal-update} gives a mechanism for an effect of the starting assignment. For a fixed torque history, the difference caused by two initial temperatures at motor $i$ decays as $[T_{i,\ell}(0)-T_{i,r}(0)]\exp(-t/\tau)$. The initial contribution combines with the loading of that motor along the route. In the closed-loop replays, changing the temperature also changes the speed command and torque cap, which can change the applied torque history. The two effects are part of the matched physical outcome under the common controller. On P3, the right-front femur motor first crossed the limit on route $(1,1)$ with the right side warm. The left-front femur motor crossed first on route $(2,2)$ with the left side warm. Both stops occurred before the junction. The mixed routes show that completing the approach alone is insufficient.

The disjoint pairs occurred in every course family and at each thermal setting. They were most frequent under the 0.44 setting and on persistent-bank courses. Each setting describes a combination of model parameters and initial temperatures. The experiment compares these tested combinations. The deadline screen excluded no forecast and every execution terminated with a finish or a thermal stop. The results characterize a thermal feasibility problem on these courses.

The heightfields are synthetic. The thermal parameters are specified simulation inputs, and the initial motor temperatures are supplied to the controllers. The absence of heat transfer between motors is an assumption of Eq.~\eqref{eq:thermal-update}. The replay audit reproduced all 18 temperature histories from the recorded torques and checked the completion and termination events. The experiment tests left--right assignments with two temperature values. Its aggregate comparator uses that prior explicitly.

\section{Conclusion}

Swapping which side started warm changed which routes the simulated hexapod could finish even with the same starting mean and maximum. On 11 of 36 matched pairs there was a finishable route for each assignment and no shared finishable route. With the same adaptive speed controller during walking, the planner given individual temperatures finished 26 of 72 starts with two thermal stops. The planner given the mean and maximum finished seven with five stops. These results establish the value of preserving the assignment of motor temperatures for route selection under the tested simulated conditions.

\section{Matched Junction-Pause Experiment}
\label{app:pause}

A separate experiment compared route selection with and without a planned 12 s pause on 12 other two-stage courses. Both arms used the adapted speed governor, the $69.50\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ forecast cutoff, the deadline, and the same route ranking. The optional arm evaluated each route at both pause durations and preferred zero pause for a tie on the same route. Both arms used the individual starting temperatures. The course lengths were 37.44--40.29 m. The deadlines used the same projected-length rule as the main experiment. The 12 courses used both warm sides at each of the three thermal settings, giving 72 starts per arm. Each arm shared the same two-second online budget as part of this earlier pause protocol.

At the flat junction in a paused replay, the controller commanded zero forward speed and the legs continued to step. The pause counted toward the deadline. The forecast held its forward coordinate fixed, applied the fitted motor loads for 24 prediction steps, and advanced the governor clock. After the pause it resumed progress with the evolved temperatures and speed state. The forecast peak included approach, pause, and departure.

For each of eight recorded 12 s pause windows on one development approach, four per approach speed, we used the simulated entry and exit temperatures, $T_{i,\mathrm{in}}$ and $T_{i,\mathrm{out}}$, to estimate the constant squared torque $q_{i,\mathrm{hold}}^2$ for a pause of duration $d$.

\begin{equation}
q_{i,\mathrm{hold}}^2 =
\frac{q_{\mathrm{ref}}^2}{R}
\left[T_{i,\mathrm{in}}-T_{\mathrm{amb}}+
\frac{T_{i,\mathrm{out}}-T_{i,\mathrm{in}}}{1-\exp(-d/\tau)}\right].
\end{equation}

We averaged inferred squared torques within each approach speed and then weighted the two speeds equally. The paused forecasts used those fitted loads (Table~\ref{tab:hold-loads}). Equation~\eqref{eq:thermal-update} predicts cooling for a motor when its junction-entry temperature exceeds the equilibrium implied by the hold load. At pause exit, the forecast resumes progress with the evolved temperature vector and current speed state. It evaluates the cutoff over the entire prediction, including the initial stand and the pause.
