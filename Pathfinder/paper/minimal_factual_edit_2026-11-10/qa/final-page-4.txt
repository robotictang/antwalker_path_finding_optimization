4 Planning methods
4.1 Complete planner
There  are  three  stages  of  CLRP.  The  first  
investigates initial corridors in the context of time,  
battery and terrain constraints. A corridor is a first-
passage through free space that serves as a path from  
which alternatives are developed in later stages. The  
second  stage  involves  the  addition  of  connections  
through  points  around  the  obstacles  and  terrain  
boundaries. Final stage is route adaptation with the  
removal of internal points, point shift towards their  
projections  on  adjacent  segments,  replacement  of  
points by midpoints and shift of point coordinates. The 
highest ranked route is kept for the entire search and  
only changed when the rank of a candidate improves. 
Routes must meet the common segment-clearance test 
prior to costing. Route geometries that are repeated re-
use cached evaluations. The same rules do also apply  
to the last local-adjustment stage of the planners that  
have been evaluated. The time fractions of CLRP,  
landmark cap, size of local moves and the parameters  
for initial searches are represented in Table 1. These  
settings restrict what is taken into account during a  
trial. Graph connection and a full route evaluation are  
performed separately. Before a line segment is added  
to a finished route, it is possible for graph search to  
discard it. Alternatives with varying resource costs and 
heading are also left over for graph search. Local-
adjustment  stage  –  alters  an  entire  series  of  route 
points. A common limit on full route evaluations does 
not imply equal numbers of geometric queries.  The  
entire CLRP process is outlined in Algorithm 1. All  
the stages are evaluated by the same evaluator and the 
same  ranking  rule.  Route  evaluations  during  
initialization count toward the fixed common ceiling.

Algorithm 1. Complete CLRP route search.
Search for routes on CLRP. Inputs: known obstacle 
geometry, terrain, start, destination, and initial 
heading, task constraints, fixed return allowance, 
random seed, and 2 s search-time limit, 240-route-
evaluation limit, and 40,000-expansion limit.
1. Perform the first (resource-aware) refiner for up to 
40% of the nominal search time. Use the common 
limit for its route assessments and keep its best 
available route. If budget allows
2. Test the direct start to destination route. Ignore bad 
or rejected geometry. Use common metrics and 
constraint-ranking rule to evaluate only valid 
routes, reusing cached evaluations for repeated 
geometry.
3. Gather internal points of the initial route, inflated 
obstacle corners and points around terrain 
boundaries. Eliminate out-of-bounds and blocked 
points and duplicate points. Order the rest of the 
candidates by the sum of their distances to the start 
and destination. Keep a maximum of 44 points, 
including the start and destination.
4. Construct collision free visibility links. Explore the 
resulting graph with heading-aware states, in turn 
by score, time and battery. Do not exceed 40,000 
(minus expansions used by the initial refiner) and 
32% nominal time for this stage. Use common 
ranking rule to evaluate full routes.
5. Test the best retained route with internal-point 
deletion, movement halfway towards the 
neighbouring-segment projection, midpoint 
replacement as well as moves in eight directions at 
step size of 0.75, 0.25 and 0.08 m. Keep only 
improvements in rank. At every step size, 
terminate after 3 passes (or no improvement pass 
or shared budget is exhausted). 
Output the best route retained and metrics, or initial 
search status if no route is retained. Rejected routes 
are never the first ones used when compared to 
accepted routes.
4.2 PSO and ant controls
PSO-P and ACO-P choose subsequences of points  
from the initial corridors, and carry out the common  
final  local-adjustment  stage.  The  parameters,  for  
population  sizes,  update  coefficients  and  iteration  
limits, are listed in table 1. The internal path points of 
PSO-C are represented by continuous coordinates. It  
begins  by  performing  the  same  configured  initial  
corridor search as CLRP. The longest segments are  
divided in half until there are at least 6 interior points  
in the path. Corners are maintained in the subdivision.
