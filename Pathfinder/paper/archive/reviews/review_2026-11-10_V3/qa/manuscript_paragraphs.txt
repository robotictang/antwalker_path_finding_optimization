P001 Constrained Route Planning for a Simulated Hexapod
P002 Jin Wayne Foowaynejinfoo123@gmail.com
P004 Abstract
P005 In this work, we evaluate landmark search and final local modification in body-route planning for a simulated hexapod. Six configurations share obstacle-clearance, deadline, nominal battery and terrain-exposure constraints. Parameter selection consists of 64 trials over eight development maps; the main test consists of 576 trials over 32 maps, giving 640 planning trials. The complete planner, Constrained Landmark Route Planning (CLRP), accepts 93 of 96 main-test attempts, compared with 94 for coordinate-based particle swarm optimization, PSO-C. Removing final adjustment reduces acceptance to 90. Without landmark search, 93 acceptances are retained and mean search time falls from 0.376 to 0.196 s. Complete CLRP has a conditional mean paired-map relative score improvement of 0.924% over this reduced version. Against PSO-C, the mean paired-map relative score improvement is 0.690%, with a zero median and a descriptive 95% bootstrap interval of [−0.255, 2.312]%. This interval includes zero and does not demonstrate a mean score advantage. A separate diagnostic consists of 16 executions: four missions under four assistance conditions. Full and half-scale assistance each complete all four missions. Both zero-assistance conditions complete none and time out at 180 s. Assisted executions record no foot contact under the six-foot contact test. The results quantify planning trade-offs and dependence on direct body assistance. Nominal battery costs are model outputs without physical or electrical validation.
P006 Keywords: Hexapod; planning; optimization; simulation; reproducibility
P007 1 Introduction
P008 A robot's path must avoid obstacles and satisfy the conditions of its task. A short route can still miss a deadline or leave insufficient battery reserve to reach a charging station. The current model accounts for terrain-dependent walking speeds, the time and nominal battery use associated with turns, and limits on exposure to difficult terrain. Route selection must account for these costs and separately verify that all task requirements are satisfied.
P009 This study investigates the impact of landmark search and final local route adjustments on acceptance, route score and computation in MuJoCo [1], while controlling for map, evaluator and task constraints. The full planner, CLRP, includes initial corridor search, additional links around obstacles and terrain boundaries, and local refinement of intermediate route coordinates. The analysis includes particle swarm and ant-inspired planners that choose points from initial routes, a particle swarm planner that changes the coordinates of intermediate points, and two versions of CLRP with one search stage removed. The experiment evaluates the implemented configurations; it does not propose a new swarm update rule or establish universal planner superiority.
P010 The study distinguishes three outcomes: acceptance under resource constraints, score differences among attempts accepted by both methods, and the computation needed to find routes. We use eight development maps to select configurations before the main evaluation on 32 maps with three paired repetitions. Stage-removal comparisons assess whether additional search improves an accepted route or changes acceptance. Four separate maps are used to examine reliance on direct body assistance, distinguishing route acceptance from validation of locomotion.
P011 The hexapod application determines the two-dimensional body-clearance envelope and the assumptions about walking speed and turning. The planner receives the map and pose directly and plans body trajectories rather than full sequences of foot placements. The results apply to the specified geometric and nominal resource model. This framework allows consistent evaluation of the planning stages. Nominal battery costs and assisted movement are not measurements of physical robot performance.
P012 2 Related Literature
P013 A* explores a graph using the sum of the cost to reach a point and an estimate of the remaining cost [2]. Particle swarm optimization updates candidate solutions using particle motion and the best positions found by individual particles and the swarm [3]. The ant system uses pheromone and heuristic information to guide route construction [4]. A visibility graph connects points with unobstructed straight segments [5]. These established methods inform the initial search, population updates and graph connections used here. The constrained search keeps its best candidate, but that candidate is not necessarily globally optimal.
P014 Routing under resource constraints involves more than minimizing the length of one route. Ma integrates A* and label setting for multimodal routes under resource and mode-sequence constraints [6]. The transportation model describes related constrained search techniques, but its network and mode-chain problems differ from the planar robot paths studied here. In the present method, a finite set of initial search labels and landmark candidates is followed by continuous point refinement. A single evaluator assesses deadlines, nominal battery allowances for the outward and return routes, and terrain exposure.
P015 Guo et al. [7] propose a hybrid approach to mobile-robot trajectory planning based on particle swarm optimization and control-barrier-function velocity adjustments. Their safety mechanism ties planning to speed control. The current comparison retains the segment-clearance test and compares acceptance and score under the same resource constraints. It does not implement or evaluate their controller. Changing the collision model, initialization or execution method affects both the comparison and the optimizer.
P016 Hexapod research also addresses motion below the body-route level. Yin et al. optimize swing trajectories, gait timing and gait sequence on LittleStrong [8]. Chen et al. integrate terrain features with traversal costs, foot placement and locomotion strategy in simulation and physical experiments [9]. The current work holds the gait controller fixed, supplies terrain data directly and investigates the removal of body-route planning stages. Its separate assistance diagnostic does not replace the physical and foot-placement assessments in those studies.
P017 These studies lead to a specific experimental question: how do acceptance and route score change when landmark search and final adjustment are added under a common evaluator? Complete configurations are compared because their initialization procedures and route representations differ. They are not replicas of every referenced planner. The research documents these choices and counts both route evaluations and geometric queries, following the COCO procedure’s emphasis on initialization, parameter selection and termination criteria [10]. The comparison applies to the specified implementations and resource model.
P018 3 Robot and planning model
P019 3.1 Robot model and body assistance
P020 The robot model has six legs, each with three articulated joints. The imported model has a total mass of 97.52 kg and a base mass of 35 kg. The assembled MuJoCo model has a total robot mass of 113.32 kg and a base mass of 46 kg. The assembly includes wheels and a scanner, giving 23 actuators in total. All experiments use walking mode; the wheels and scanner remain part of the simulated mass.
P021 Figure 1 shows the MuJoCo robot in a static view of its walking configuration. The fixed viewpoint shows the assembly, including the wheels and scanner. Nearby obstacles are hidden for clarity. The planning results in Section 6 use the maps and task constraints described in Section 5.
P024 Figure 1. Static view of the simulated hexapod in its walking configuration. The wheels and scanner remain part of the assembly.
P026 The imported robot specification lists leg-joint limits of 60 Nm torque and 20 rad/s angular velocity. These are declared limits in the specification. The study includes no physical measurements of motor torque-speed characteristics, battery depletion or robot mass. The assembled simulation is the platform evaluated here; the imported mass values distinguish the source model from the assembled model.
P027 MuJoCo advances the physics in 0.004 s steps. The motion controller runs once every four physics steps, or every 0.016 s. Gravitational acceleration is 9.81 m/s². In the standard configuration, gravity compensation is enabled on 18 leg bodies. Gravity compensation and direct body assistance are separate mechanisms: the former offsets gravity on selected bodies, while the latter directly changes the free body’s velocity.
P028 Body assistance constrains vertical motion and roll/pitch rates once per controller frame. It also changes horizontal velocity and yaw rate at each physics step. At assistance scale 1, horizontal velocity is set to the requested velocity. At scale 0.5, each correction retains 50% of the current velocity and adds 50% of the requested velocity. This blend is applied four times per controller frame. At scale 0, the assistance operation leaves these body velocities unchanged. The scale specifies velocity blending rather than a support force.
P029 The planner obtains ground-truth position, heading, obstacle geometry and terrain information directly from the simulator. These inputs are not estimated from sensor measurements. The start, initial heading, destination and estimated return to the charging station are held constant across methods for each trial. The study therefore compares route choices on the same known map. The input model excludes sensor noise, localization errors and map reconstruction.
P030 3.2 Route geometry
P031 A path P is a sequence of points on the horizontal plane. The first and last points are the specified start and destination. The points are connected by straight segments in order. The first search grid has a spacing of 0.25 m and eight possible directions of motion. Diagonal moves through blocked grid corners are prohibited.
P032 Every returned segment must pass the same clearance test against the planar obstacle geometry. The robot radius is set to 1.82 m, the tracking margin to 0.12 m and the segment-clearance tolerance to 0.005 m. These values define the planning clearance model. The analysis considers a path for the robot body and does not compute individual three-dimensional foot-placement trajectories.
P033 The terrain map defines regions with different walking-speed limits and difficulty values. Equations (1) and (2) calculate terrain difficulty at each grid location.
P034 d = clip(g, 0, 2)(1)
P035 g = 14(s0.25 + h0.12 + u0.04 + f)(2)
P036 Here, the clip function restricts its first argument to [0, 2]. The variables s, h and u denote slope in radians, height range in meters, and the population standard deviation of sampled ground heights in meters, respectively; f denotes semantic softness. Heights are sampled at nine positions using offsets of −1.82, 0 and 1.82 m along each horizontal axis. Slope is the arctangent of the gradient magnitude of the mean sampled height, computed on the 0.25 m grid. For the tested terrain grades 0, 1 and 2, f equals 0, 0.25 and 0.60, respectively. Terrain-grade queries include a 1.1 m margin around patch boundaries. Difficulty is sampled at intervals of no more than 0.125 m along each route segment. Each route sample uses the difficulty of its nearest grid cell. Segment exposure is the segment length multiplied by its mean sampled difficulty. Total exposure R is the sum across segments. This quantity is calculated from the terrain map and does not represent an estimated probability of slipping.
P037 3.3 Route score and acceptance
P038 The route evaluator calculates distance D in meters, predicted outward travel time T in seconds, nominal battery use E in percentage points, and terrain exposure R. The initial heading is π/2 radians. Predicted time includes travel at terrain-dependent speed limits, heading changes at 1 rad/s and an acceleration allowance. Speed limits for smooth, mild and higher-grade terrain are 0.75, 0.65 and 0.38 m/s, respectively.
P039 Equation (3) combines the four quantities into a score Q. Lower scores are better. The denominators express one score unit as 50 m of distance, 90 s of predicted time, 30 nominal battery percentage points or 80 exposure units. These fixed trade-offs were not derived from robot measurements or fitted to the main-test results.
P040 Q(P) = D50 + T90 + E30 + R80(3)
P041 Acceptance means that the predicted arrival time is within the deadline, exposure is within the terrain limit, and the required nominal battery allowance is within the available battery. Equation (4) defines the required allowance. The subscript return denotes the nominal battery consumption of a route from the destination to a charging station. The 1.25 multiplier and the additional six percentage points are fixed reserve requirements.
P042 Breq = 1.25(E + Ereturn) + 6(4)
P043 The battery cost model assigns 0.70 percentage points per walking meter, 0.015 per second, 0.08 per turning radian and a 0.10 start/stop allowance. These coefficients were not fitted to electrical measurements. Distance, travel time and turning also contribute to E, so the four score terms are not independent physical outcomes. Combining them expresses a fixed preference for these route properties. The 1.25 multiplier increases the predicted outward-and-return cost by 25%, and the six-point reserve adds a fixed allowance. Neither rule is a statistically calibrated probability of completing a physical mission. All evaluations use the same parameters. Sensitivity to different score weights or reserve settings is not tested. Battery use in this paper means the output of this equation-based model. Mechanical joint work and electrical battery consumption are different quantities; the route score does not convert one into the other.
P044 The charging station and return route are fixed once per trial before the planners are compared. Each planner then selects an outward route using the same return allowance. The destination deadline applies to the outward leg. The motion experiment follows the outward and return routes and includes eight seconds of simulated station service in completed mission times. Whole-mission distance and elapsed time describe execution of the destination-and-return task, whereas D and T describe the planned outward route. Unfinished executions can end before completing either leg.
P045 Candidate ranking first distinguishes routes that satisfy all constraints from those that do not. For each constraint, raw excesses of at most 10−8 are treated as zero. Larger positive excesses are divided by their corresponding limits and summed. Lower total normalized violation ranks first, followed by lower Q when total normalized violations are equal. Among accepted routes, lower Q ranks first. A rejected candidate cannot replace an accepted route.
P046 4 Planning methods
P047 4.1 Complete planner
P048 CLRP has three stages. The first explores initial corridors under time, battery and terrain constraints. A corridor is an initial route through free space from which later stages generate alternatives. The second stage adds connections through points around obstacles and terrain boundaries. The final stage adjusts routes by deleting internal points, moving points toward their projections onto neighboring segments, replacing points with midpoints and moving point coordinates.
P049 The best-ranked route is retained throughout the search and is replaced only when a candidate improves its rank. Proposed routes must pass the common segment-clearance test before their costs are evaluated. Repeated route geometries reuse cached evaluations. These rules also apply to the final local-adjustment stage shared by the evaluated planners.
P050 Table 1 shows the time fractions of CLRP, the landmark cap, the size of local moves and the parameters for initial searches. These settings limit the options considered in a trial.
P051 A graph connection and a full route evaluation are counted separately. A line segment can be discarded by graph search before it is included in a complete route. Graph search also retains alternatives with different headings and resource costs. The local-adjustment stage changes a whole sequence of route points. A common limit on full route evaluations therefore does not imply equal numbers of geometric queries across methods.
P052 Algorithm 1 describes the complete CLRP method. Every stage uses the same evaluator and ranking rule. Route evaluations performed during initialization count toward the common ceiling.
P053 Algorithm 1. Complete CLRP route search.
P054 Inputs: known obstacle geometry and terrain; start, destination and initial heading; task constraints and fixed return allowance; random seed; a 2 s search-time limit, a 240-route-evaluation limit and a shared 40,000-expansion limit.
P055 1. Run the initial resource-aware refiner for up to 40% of the nominal search time. Charge its route evaluations to the shared limit and retain its best available route.
P056 2. Test the direct start-to-destination route if budget remains. Reject invalid or blocked geometry. Evaluate valid routes using the common metrics and constraint-ranking rule; reuse cached evaluations for repeated geometry.
P057 3. Collect the initial route’s internal points, inflated obstacle corners and points around terrain boundaries. Remove duplicate, out-of-bounds and blocked points. Sort the remaining candidates by the sum of their distances to the start and destination. Retain at most 44 points, including the start and destination.
P058 4. Build collision-free visibility connections. Search the resulting graph with heading-aware states using score, time and battery weighting in turn. Limit this stage to its 32% nominal-time allocation and at most 40,000 minus the expansions used by the initial refiner. Evaluate complete routes with the common ranking rule.
P059 5. Adjust the best retained route by testing internal-point deletion, movement halfway toward the neighboring-segment projection, midpoint replacement and moves in eight directions at step sizes of 0.75, 0.25 and 0.08 m. Retain only improvements in rank. At each step size, stop after three passes, a pass with no improvement, or exhaustion of the shared budget.
P060 Output: the best retained route and its metrics, or the initial search status if no route is retained. Accepted routes always rank ahead of rejected routes.
P061 4.2 PSO and ant controls
P062 PSO-P and ACO-P select subsequences of points from the initial corridors and perform the common final local-adjustment stage. Table 1 lists the parameters for population sizes, update coefficients and iteration limits.
P063 PSO-C uses continuous coordinates for internal path points. It starts with the same configured initial corridor search as CLRP. The longest segments are bisected until the path has at least six internal points. The subdivision preserves the current corners. Start and end points remain fixed. The particle representation keeps a fixed number of internal points during swarm search; their x and y coordinates may change within the map.
P064 PSO-C initializes all particles except the first with Gaussian perturbations of internal point coordinates, using a standard deviation of 2 m. During swarm iterations, each coordinate update is limited to ±2 m. Particle positions are clipped to the map bounds of [−24, 24] m on each horizontal axis (Table 1). Proposals that fail the clearance test are rejected. If particle proposals do not improve the initial best route, that route remains available, and final adjustment shares the common evaluation ceiling.
P065 PSO-C and PSO-P differ in their route representations and initial-route procedures. The evaluation measures the overall effect of these implementation choices. It does not isolate the effect of representation alone. All reported results apply to the specified route objective and settings.
P066 4.3 Elimination of planning stages and search limits

In particular, noP removes only the final adjustment and keeps the operations within the initial refiner. These comparisons measure the effect of removing one stage from the specified planner. P092 5 Experimental design
P093 5.1 Maps and task limits
P094 The study uses eight development maps with seeds from 2130000 to 2130007 and 32 main-test maps with seeds from 2140000 to 2140031. The map categories alternate between open, cluttered, rough and corridor. Each category has two development maps and eight main-test maps. Each map covers −24 to 24 m along both horizontal axes.
P095 Open, cluttered, rough and corridor maps contain 5, 16, 7 and 4 generated obstacles, respectively. Corridor maps also contain two interrupted barriers. Maps in these four families contain 1, 2, 4 and 3 terrain patches, respectively. Each map defines obstacles, shallow terrain tiles and regions that determine walking speed and exposure. All six planners receive the same geometry and terrain for a given case.
P096 Start and destination proposals are sampled within [−17, 17] m on each axis with an initial separation of 12–26 m. Proposals are moved to free grid locations and retained if their final separation is at least 10 m. Deadlines are 45, 70, 110 and 160 s. Available nominal battery levels are 45%, 65%, 85% and 100%, and exposure limits are 10, 20 and 40. The return route to the charging station is estimated before the methods are compared.
P097 The nominal battery cost coefficients are identical for all maps. Changing battery allowances changes the available resource limit, not the nominal battery use assigned to a meter or a turn. The experiment changes map geometry and task constraints. It does not test a calibrated range of physical battery properties.
P098 5.2 Parameter selection and repeated tests
P099 Each of the four base methods is tested with two parameter profiles on the development maps. Selection first favors the profile with more accepted attempts. If acceptance counts are equal, the profile with the lower mean score among accepted attempts is selected. Exact ties retain the default profile. Every base method selects the default, and both reduced CLRP variants inherit that selection.
P100 The alternative PSO-P configuration uses 24 particles with inertia 0.55 and cognitive/social coefficients of 1.7 and 1.3. The alternative ACO-P configuration uses 18 ants, a heuristic exponent of 3 and an evaporation rate of 0.35. The alternative PSO-C profile uses 20 particles, 60 iterations, inertia 0.75, cognitive/social coefficients of 1.6, and a perturbation and velocity bound of 4 m. The alternative CLRP profile uses initial-search and extra-point fractions of 0.55 and 0.25, 60 points, and local move sizes of 0.50, 0.15 and 0.05 m. All other settings retain their corresponding defaults.
P101 The development phase contains 64 planning attempts: eight maps, four methods and two profiles. The main test contains 576 attempts: 32 maps, six methods and three paired repetitions. Each method therefore has 96 main-test attempts, and the full experiment has 640 attempts. The maps, parameter-selection criteria and primary analysis were defined before the main-test results. There was no external preregistration. Acceptance counts include all scheduled main-test attempts.
P102 The three repetitions on each map use the same geometry and task constraints and measure algorithmic variation within that map. They do not represent three independent maps. Paired relative score differences are first averaged across mutually accepted repetitions within each map, then summarized across maps. Each paired map therefore receives equal weight even when the number of mutually accepted repetitions differs.
P103 5.3 Score comparison and statistical analysis
P104 Score comparisons use only attempts for which both CLRP and the comparison method accept a route. Acceptance by only one method and rejection by both methods are counted separately. Equation (5) defines the relative score difference for a paired attempt. Qc denotes the comparison method’s score, and Qv denotes CLRP’s score. A positive difference means CLRP has the lower score; a negative difference means the comparison method has the lower score. Absolute differences between Qc and Qv of at most 10−8 are counted as ties.
P105 δ = 100 Qc − QvQc(5)
P106 The reported mean and median summarize the map averages of paired relative score differences. Following the resampling principle in [11], descriptive 95% percentile bootstrap intervals use 5,000 resamples of maps with random seed 41104. Repetitions remain grouped within their map. The intervals are conditional on acceptance by both methods and are not adjusted for the five comparisons. A 0.5% relative-improvement threshold is used only as a descriptive reference for counting larger improvements.
P107 Map-family breakdowns, computational summaries, map-by-map displays and omission analyses were calculated after the main test. They describe the recorded results without changing the selected settings or main-test sample. In the omission analysis, one paired map is removed at a time and the mean is recalculated. The resulting means show each map’s influence on the average. All maps remain in the primary analysis.
P108 Evaluations run on Linux with Python 3.10.12, MuJoCo 3.9.0, NumPy 2.2.6 and SciPy 1.15.3. The processor is an Intel Core i5-7600 at 3.50 GHz with four logical processors. The desktop runs concurrent tasks. Recorded wall times describe this shared-desktop setting and do not measure exclusive processor use or deployment latency.
P109 5.4 Motion tests and contact measurements
P110 The motion experiment uses four previously assessed maps: one each from the open, cluttered, rough and corridor families, with seeds 1670000–1670003. Each mission follows the CLRP route from the map’s first repetition and then the fixed return route to a charging station. Outward route lengths are 21.36, 22.30, 23.45 and 19.78 m, respectively. Initial nominal battery levels are 45%, 85%, 45% and 100%, respectively. These first-repetition routes form a separate diagnostic from the 32 main-test maps.
P111 Each mission is executed under four conditions: full body assistance with gravity compensation, half-scale assistance with compensation, zero assistance with compensation, and zero assistance without compensation. All conditions use the same path tracker and a maximum simulated execution time of 180 s. Completion requires reaching the destination and returning to the station. Unfinished missions time out at 180 s; this execution limit is distinct from the planner’s outward-route deadline. Completed mission times include eight seconds of simulated station service.
P112 Gravity compensation is verified after initialization of the locomotion controller. The no-compensation condition sets all leg-body compensation values to zero at that point. Four initial executions in which initialization restored compensation were excluded and rerun after its removal was verified. The final comparison contains 16 executions: four missions under each of four conditions. These previously assessed routes provide a diagnostic of the execution model rather than a new test set for locomotion.
P113 Foot contact is logged at every 0.004 s physics step. Each of the six foot geometries is counted as contacting an external geometry when the reported contact distance is at most 0.002 m. Mean contact feet is the average number of feet detected in contact per sample. The no-contact fraction is the fraction of samples with no detected foot contacts. The detector covers only the six feet; contacts involving other robot components are excluded from these counts.
P114 Body tilt is calculated from body orientation. Assisted executions directly adjust roll and pitch angular velocities using orientation feedback, so low tilt describes the assisted controller’s behavior. Positive actuator work is calculated by multiplying actuator force by actuator velocity, retaining only positive power, integrating over time and summing over actuators. It excludes work introduced by direct changes to body velocity. These metrics describe the model’s motion and actuator work under the specified assistance condition.

P244 7 Discussion
P245 Final local adjustment improves acceptance and yields lower scores in more paired accepted attempts than landmark search under the tested settings. Removing it loses three acceptances on corridor maps; removing landmark search preserves acceptance and reduces recorded computation. Incumbent retention permits only improvements in rank, so a retained route cannot worsen during the final stage. The empirical result quantifies the trade-offs under the selected settings. Separately timed stage-removal trials do not guarantee identical search histories across repetitions. An initial-route-only baseline and a matched-budget comparison would help separate initialization quality from the contribution of later stages; those comparisons are not included here.
P246 The study does not demonstrate a mean score advantage for CLRP over PSO-C. The mean paired-map relative score improvement is 0.690%, but its descriptive 95% bootstrap interval includes zero and the median is zero. An interval spanning zero establishes neither equality nor inferiority. PSO-C accepts one additional attempt, and map 24 strongly influences the positive mean relative improvement. The CLRP-versus-PSO-P and CLRP-versus-ACO-P comparisons have positive mean relative improvements and zero median paired-map effects; these results apply to the tested configurations. Differences in initialization and route representation prevent attribution to representation alone. The five intervals are unadjusted for multiple comparisons and are descriptive rather than confirmatory evidence.
P247 All methods accept every tested non-corridor attempt, so acceptance differences are confined to corridor instances within the four specified map families. The three repetitions measure algorithmic variation within maps and do not increase the number of independent main-test maps beyond 32. More complex or unknown environments require a separate evaluation. Sensitivity to score weights and reserve settings is not evaluated. The combined score contains overlapping route preferences because nominal battery use also depends on distance and duration. Actuator work excludes work introduced by direct body assistance, and no electrical measurements validate physical battery savings. Search times describe the recorded shared-desktop setting and exclude shared preparation; they are not deployment latency measurements.
P248 The motion diagnostic establishes dependence on direct body assistance for the four tested missions. Assistance corrects body velocities and roll/pitch rates, so completed missions and low tilt do not independently validate a walking gait. The six-foot contact measurements further limit that interpretation. Known map and pose inputs, different source and assembled masses, and the absence of physical motor, battery, terrain and contact measurements define the simulation’s scope. Physical walking and sensor-based navigation are not evaluated.
P249 8 Conclusions
P250 On 32 main-test maps, final local refinement increases acceptance from 90 to 93 of 96 attempts. Landmark search preserves acceptance and gives a conditional mean paired-map relative score improvement of 0.924% compared with its removal, while increasing mean search time from 0.196 to 0.376 s. The two stage-removal comparisons therefore show different trade-offs between acceptance, score and computation. The study does not demonstrate a mean score advantage over PSO-C: the mean paired-map relative improvement is 0.690%, the median is zero, and the descriptive 95% bootstrap interval includes zero. PSO-C accepts one additional attempt.
P251 The 16 valid motion executions provide a separate diagnostic. Full and half-scale assistance each complete all four missions. Both zero-assistance conditions complete none and time out at 180 s, with or without verified leg-body gravity compensation. All assisted executions record no foot contact under the defined six-foot test. The study therefore distinguishes acceptance of a constrained body route from validation of unassisted locomotion. The reported costs and trade-offs apply to the specified simulation and objective.
