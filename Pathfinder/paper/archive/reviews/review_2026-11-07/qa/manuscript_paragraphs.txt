P003 [P2] Abstract
P004 [P3] In this work, we evaluate landmark identification and final local modification in body route navigation for a simulated hexapod . There are six configurations with the same obstacle clearance, deadline, nominal battery and terrain exposure parameters. The parameter selection consisted of 64 trials over 8 development maps. The main test consisted of 576 trials over 32 maps, for a total of 640 planning trials. The comprehensive planner Constrained Landmark Route Planning (CLRP) succeeds in 93 out of 96 primary test attempts, compared to 94 for coordinate-based particle swarm optimization, PSO-C. If the last adjustment is removed, acceptance falls to 90. Without landmark search, 93 acceptances are retained and the average search duration is reduced from 0.376 seconds to 0.196 seconds. The full CLRP results in a conditional mean paired-map relative score increase of 0.924% compared to this short version. The paired map had an average relative score improvement of 0.690% over PSO-C, with a median of zero and a 95% bootstrap confidence interval of [−0.255, 2.312]%. This range includes zero and does not indicate a better average score. A separate diagnostic consists of 16 runs: four tasks in four support conditions. All four objectives are met by comprehensive and partial support Zero-assistance conditions do not complete and time out at 180 seconds. Assisted executions show no foot contact in the six foot contact assessment. The results quantify the tradeoffs in planning and dependence on direct physical support. The nominal battery costs are results of models not validated physically or electrically.

P006 [P2] 1 Introduction
P007 [P5] A robot's path must avoid obstacles and satisfy the conditions of its task. A short route can still miss a deadline or have insufficient battery capacity to reach a charging station. The current model takes into account the fact that the terrain can affect the allowed walking speed, that turns can increase the time and nominal battery usage and that the encounters with difficult terrain are limited. Taking into account expenses in route selection and independently verifying all task requirements.
P008 [P5] This study investigates the impact of landmark search and final local route adjustments on acceptance, route evaluation, and computation in MuJoCo [1], while controlling for map, evaluator, and task limitations. The full planner, CLRP includes initial corridor search, additional links around obstacles and terrain edges, and local refinement of intermediate route coordinates. The analysis includes a particle swarm and an ant-inspired planner which choose points from initial trajectories, a particle swarm planner which changes the coordinates of intermediate points, and two versions of CLRP with one search phase removed. The experiment is an evaluation of the existing configurations and not a proposal of a new swarm update rule or a planner that is always better.
P009 [P5] The study found 3 results, acceptance within the limits of resources, variation of the score based on mutual acceptance through both methods and the calculations needed to calculate the routes. We use eight development maps for configuration determination before the main evaluation on 32 maps with three paired iterations. Stage-removal comparisons assess if supplementary searches improve an approved route or change its acceptance. Four different maps are used to examine reliance on direct bodily support, differentiating between route acceptance and verification of locomotion.
P010 [P5] The assumptions about walking velocity, and rotation, and the two-dimensional body-clearance envelope are determined by the hexapod application. The planner directly receives the map and pose and plans body trajectories instead of full sequences of foot placements. The results are in accordance with the prescribed geometric and nominal resource regime. This framework allows a consistent evaluation of the planning steps. Nominal battery costs and assisted movement are not measures of actual robot performance.
P011 [P2] 2 Related Literature
P012 [P6] A* explores a graph based on the sum of the cost to reach a point and an estimate of the cost remaining [2]. Particle swarm optimisation improves possible solutions by leveraging the movement of particles, and the optimal positions found by individual particles and the collective swarm [3]. The ant system uses pheromonal and heuristic information to guide the formation of routes [4]. A visibility graph connects points by free line segments [5]. These established methods inform the preliminary search, demographic updates, and graph interconnections used here. The restricted search keeps its best candidate, but it is not necessarily globally optimal.
P014 [P6] Routing under resource constraints is more than simply minimising the length of one route. Ma integrates A* and label setting for multimodal routes under a sequence of resource and mode-sequence constraints [6]. Related constrained search techniques are introduced by the transportation model, but its network and mode-chain problems are different from the planar robot trajectories studied here. Here a small set of initial search labels and landmarks are followed by continuous refinement of points, with a single evaluator determining due dates, battery return capacity and terrain.
P015 [P6] Guo et al. [7] propose a hybrid approach for mobile robots trajectory planning based on particle swarm optimization and control barrier function velocity adjustments. Their safety mechanism ties planning to speed control. The current comparison retains the segment-clearance test, and compares acceptance and score under the same resource constraints. It does not run, or evaluate, their controller. This is important because changing the collision model, initialization or execution method will affect both the comparison and the optimizer.
P016 [P6] Also the hexapod investigations lead to better measurements below the body-route level. Yin et al. improve swing trajectories, gait timing and gait sequence on LittleStrong [8]. Chen et al. integrate topographical characteristics with travel expenses, foot positioning and movement strategies in both simulated and experimental studies [9]. The current work presents a static gait controller, the input of terrain data and the investigation of elimination of body-route phases. Its individual assistance evaluation outcomes are not substitutes for the physical and foot-placement Examinations in those studies.
P017 [P6] These questions boil down to a specific experimental question: how the acceptance and route scores change when we add the landmark search and the final adjustments under a single evaluator. The comprehensive configurations are compared as they have different initialization processes and route representations. They aren’t duplicates of each and every planner referenced. The research describes such decisions throughout both route assessments and geometric queries, in line with the emphasis on initialization, parameter selection and termination criteria of the COCO procedure [10]. The contrast is about the assigned implementations and resource framework.

The gravity compensation and direct body assistance are two different mechanisms: the former compensates the effects of gravity on certain bodies, and the latter directly changes the velocity of the free body.
P026 [P5] Body assistance limits vertical movement and roll/pitch rates once every controller cycle. It also changes the horizontal velocity and the yaw rate with each iteration of the physics. At assistance level 1 the horizontal speed is adjusted to the desired speed. An adjustment at scale 0.5 maintains 50% of the current speed while adding 50% of the desired speed. The mixture is used four times per controller frame. The support operation at scale 0 preserves these body velocities. The scale is a measure of the amalgamation of velocities, not a force of support.
P027 [P5] The planner obtains the exact position, orientation, obstacle dimensions and terrain information directly from the simulator. These inputs are not based on sensor readings. All methods start from the same starting point, in the same main direction, to the same end point and estimated return to the charging station in each trial. The study therefore looks at route choices on an identical, known map. Sensor noise, localisation errors and map reconstruction are not considered by the input model.

P044 [P2] 4 Planning methods
P045 [P7] 4.1 Complete planner
P046 [P5] There are three phases of CLRP. The first study examines initial routes constrained by time, battery life, and terrain. The corridor is an initial pathway through open space and later phases create options. The next step is to add connections using waypoints around obstacles and terrain boundaries. In the last step the routes are modified by deleting internal points, moving points to their projections on neighbouring segments, replacing points by their midpoints and changing the point coordinates.
P047 [P5] The best path is maintained throughout the search and a contender only replaces the maintained path if it improves the position. Only those proposed pathways that pass the shared segment-clearance assessment will be evaluated for expenses. Identical route configurations use stored assessments. Also subject to these regulations is the final local-adjustment phase that is common to the assessed planners.
P048 [P5] Table 1 shows temporal divisions of CLRP and the remarkable cap, dimensions of local movements and parameters for preliminary searches. These configurations restrict the options tested in an experiment.
P049 [P5] The graph connection and the full route assessment are considered independently. The graph search could eliminate a line segment before incorporating it into a full path. The graph search also keeps options with different titles and resource expenditure. But the local-adjustment phase changes a whole sequence of route markers. A frequent shortcoming of extensive route evaluations does not necessarily imply equal amounts of geometric enquiries among techniques.
P050 [P5] Algorithm 1 describes the comprehensive CLRP methodology. The same assessor and ranking criteria are used for each phase. The route assessments made in the initialization phase partly determine the limit.
P051 [P12] Algorithm 1. Complete CLRP route search.
P052 [P13] Inputs are a given obstacle configuration and landscape, a start and an end point and an initial heading, operational constraints and a given return margin, a stochastic seed, a search time limit of 2 seconds, a maximum of 240 route evaluations, and an overall expansion limit of 40,000.
P053 [P14] 1. Run the initial resource-aware refiner for up to 40% of the nominal search time. Charge its route evaluations to the shared limit and retain its best available route.
P054 [P14] 2. Test the direct start-to-destination route if budget remains. Reject invalid or blocked geometry. Evaluate valid routes using the common metrics and constraint-ranking rule; reuse cached evaluations for repeated geometry.
P055 [P14] 3. Collect the initial route’s internal points, inflated obstacle corners and points around terrain boundaries. Remove duplicate, out-of-bounds and blocked points. Sort the remaining candidates by the sum of their distances to the start and destination. Retain at most 44 points, including the start and destination.
P056 [P14] 4. Build collision-free visibility connections. Search the resulting graph with heading-aware states using score, time and battery weighting in turn. Limit this stage to its 32% nominal-time allocation and at most 40,000 minus the expansions used by the initial refiner. Evaluate complete routes with the common ranking rule.
P057 [P14] 5. Adjust the best retained route by testing internal-point deletion, movement halfway toward the neighboring-segment projection, midpoint replacement and moves in eight directions at step sizes of 0.75, 0.25 and 0.08 m. Retain only improvements in rank. At each step size, stop after three passes, a pass with no improvement, or exhaustion of the shared budget.
P058 [P15] Output: the best retained route and its metrics, or the initial search status if no route is retained. Accepted routes always rank ahead of rejected routes.
P059 [P7] 4.2 PSO and ant controls
P060 [P5] PSO-P and ACO-P select subsequences of points from the original corridors and execute the common final local improvement stage. Parameters for population size, update coefficients and iteration thresholds are listed in Table 1.
P061 [P5] PSO-C represents its internal path points in continuous coordinates. It first does the same configured initial corridor search as CLRP. The longest of these sections are broken up until the path contains at least six internal points. Current corners stay in the division. Starting and ending points are provided. In the particle representation, the number of internal points is fixed in the swarm search, but their x and y coordinates can vary over the map.
P062 [P5] PSO-C initializes by adding Gaussian perturbations to the internal point coordinates of all particles except the first one with a standard deviation of 2 m. Swarm iterations each coordinate adjustment is limited to ±2 meters. The particle positions are constrained to the map limits [−24, 24] m in each horizontal direction (Table 1). Any proposal that does not pass the clearance assessment is rejected. If the particle suggestions do not improve the initial optimal path, the initial optimal path is still available, and the final adjustment follows the usual evaluation cut-off.
P063 [P5] PSO-C and PSO-P differ in the way of representing routes and the way of establishing the initial routes. The evaluation looks at the overall effect of these implementation choices. It does not control for the effect of representation alone. All results displayed are relevant to the route objective and the parameters entered.

P090 [P2] 5 Experimental design
P091 [P7] 5.1 Maps and task limits
P092 [P5] The research employs 8 development maps with seeds from 2130000 to 2130007 and 32 main test maps with seeds from 2140 to 2140031. The map classifications are alternating between open, cluttered, rough and corridor. There are 2 development maps and 8 main test maps in each category. Each map covers −24 to 24 m along each horizontal axis.
P093 [P5] The open, disorganized, rugged and corridor maps have 5, 16, 7 and 4 generated impediments respectively. The corridor maps also feature two discontinuous barriers. The maps in these four categories have 1, 2, 4 and 3 terrain segments respectively. Each map features set obstacles, squares of shallow terrain, and areas that affect both movement speed and exposure. All six planners are given the same geometry and terrain for a given scenario.
P094 [P5] We sample proposals for start and destination in the range of [−17, 17] m on each axis, keeping an initial distance of 12-26 m. The proposals are moved to the empty positions in the grid and are kept if the final distance is more than 10 meters. The time limits are 45, 70, 110 and 160 seconds. Nominal battery levels are 45%, 65%, 85%, and 100%, and exposure thresholds are 10, 20, and 40. The methods are compared in terms of the estimated return path to the charging station.
P095 [P5] The nominal coefficients of the battery costs are the same in all maps. Changing battery allowances is a change to the resource limit, not to the amount of nominal battery consumption allocated to a meter or a turn. The experiment alters the geometric layout of the map and the parameters of the task. It does not assess a standardized spectrum of physical properties of batteries.
P096 [P7] 5.2 Parameter selection and repeated tests
P097 [P5] Each of the four basic techniques is tested with two parameter settings on the development maps. The selection process begins by choosing the profile with the highest number of accepted attempts . If the acceptance counts are tied, the profile with the lesser average score of accepted attempts is selected. Exact ties standard profile Each basic method takes the default and both modified CLRP variants use that selection.
P098 [P5] The other configuration of PSO-P uses 24 particles with inertia 0.55 and cognitive/social coefficients 1.7 and 1.3. In the alternative configuration ACO-P uses 18 ants, a heuristic exponent of 3 and an evaporation of 0.35. The other PSO-C configuration includes 20 particles, 60 iterations, inertia = 0.75, cognitive and social coefficients = 1.6 and perturbation and velocity limit = 4 m. The alternative CLRP profile uses fractions of 0.55 for initial-search and 0.25 for extra-point, 60 points, and local move dimensions of 0.50, 0.15, and 0.05 m. All other configurations default to their previous values.
P099 [P5] In the development stage there are 64 planning tasks: eight maps, four methodologies and two profiles. The main test consists of 576 trials: 32 maps, 6 techniques and 3 paired repetitions. Each technique includes 96 primary test attempts, and the entire experiment consists of 640 attempts. The maps, the criteria for selecting the parameters, and the initial analysis were defined prior to the results of the main test. No external pre-registration. All planned primary examination work is included in acceptance totals.
P100 [P5] All three runs on each map use the same geometry and task restrictions, measuring the algorithmic variation on that particular map. They don't mean three separate maps. For paired comparison relative score differences, we first average within each map across mutually agreed repetitions, and then we average across maps. Therefore, each corresponding map is given equal importance regardless of the variation in the number of jointly recognised repetitions.

P107 [P7] 5.4 Motion tests and contact measurements
P108 [P5] The motion experiment uses four already evaluated maps, one from each of the open, cluttered, rough and corridor categories respectively, with seeds 1670-1670003. Each mission follows the CLRP pathway from the first iteration of the map and then the instructed return pathway to a charging station. The outward routes measure 21.36 m, 22.30 m, 23.45 m and 19.78 m in length respectively. Tentative nominal battery percentages are 45%, 85%, 45% and 100% respectively. P109 [P5] Each mission is conducted under four conditions: full bodily support with gravitational compensation, partial-scale support with compensation, no support with compensation, and no support without compensation. All scenarios have the same path tracker and maximum simulated execution time of 180 seconds. Fulfilment requires arriving at the destination and then returning to the station. The external route deadline of the planner is independent of the execution constraint that incomplete tasks expire after 180 seconds. Mission completion times are simulated station operations of eight seconds.
P110 [P5] Once the locomotion controller is initialised, we check the gravity compensation. At that point, the no-compensation condition sets all leg-body compensation values to zero. The first four executions, where initialisation restored compensation, were omitted and rerun after confirming that compensation was removed. Finally, the comparison includes 16 runs, four missions for each of the four conditions. The pathways evaluated previously provide an analysis of the execution framework, not a new testing array for movement.
P111 [P5] Each physics interval of 0.004 second captures foot contact. If the measured contact distance is less than 0.002 m, then any of the six foot geometries are deemed to be in contact with an external geometry. Mean contact feet is the average number of feet found in contact for each sample. The no-contact fraction is the percent of samples where no foot contacts are found. The detector only picks up the six feet. Counts do not include interactions with other parts of the robot.
P112 [P5] The body tilt is calculated from the body alignment. Assisted executions modify directly the roll and pitch angular velocities by using a feedback of orientation.The assisted controller behaviour is characterised by a low tilt. Positive actuator work is computed by multiplying actuator force times actuator velocity . This only takes positive power, which is integrated over time and summed over all actuators . It does not include work due to instantaneous changes in the velocity of the body. These metrics characterise the model motion and actuator performance under the specified assistance parameters.

P242 [P2] 7 Discussion
P243 [P5] The final local modification improves acceptance and leads to better scores on more paired accepted trials than landmark search given the parameters tested. Taking it out loses three acceptances on corridor maps, whereas skipping landmark search keeps the acceptance but reduces the computation recorded. The retention of incumbents allows for improvements in ranking only, so a retained route cannot get worse in the final phase. The empirical result measures the trade-offs among the parameters selected. Independent stage removal scheduling does not guarantee uniform search histories across iterations. A baseline using the initial route and a budget-matched comparison would help to separate the quality of initialisation from the impact of later phases; such comparisons are not made here.
P244 [P5] The research does not show superiority of mean scores from CLRP over PSO-C. The average gain in the paired-map relative score is 0.690% but the median is still zero and the 95% bootstrap confidence interval contains zero. If a range contains zero, that does not imply equality or subordination. PSO-C permits one more trial and the map 24 has great impact on the favourable mean relative improvement. The PSO-P versus ACO-P comparisons show positive mean relative improvements and no median paired-map effects, which are the results of the configurations tested. Differences in initialisation and route representation prevent attributing all differences to representation alone. The five intervals are not corrected for multiple comparisons and thus are descriptive rather than confirmatory.
P245 [P5] All techniques support all non-corridor tasks evaluated, therefore differences in acceptance are limited to corridor tasks within the four defined map classes. The three repetitions are a measure of diversity in maps by algorithm and do not contribute to the number of independent primary test maps beyond 32. More complex or less common settings must be assessed on a case-by-case basis. No sensitivity assessment is performed on score weights and reserve configurations. The aggregate score accounts for intersecting route preferences, as nominal battery consumption is affected by both distance and duration. The operation of the actuator does not involve body-assisted work, and there are no electrical tests that support physical battery conservation. Search times are for the documented shared-desktop configuration and do not include shared preparation; search times are not a measure of deployment latency.
P246 [P5] Dependence on direct body support is shown in motion diagnostic across the four assessed tasks. Finalized missions and minimal tilt do not independently support a walking gait. Support modulates body speeds and roll/pitch rates. The contact measurements of six feet provided additional constraints on that interpretation. The simulation parameters are based on known map and pose inputs, a range of source and combined mass, and no physical motor, battery, terrain, and contact metrics. This assessment does not involve walking or sensor-based navigation.
P247 [P2] 8 Conclusions
P248 [P5] The final local refinement improves acceptance from 90 to 93 out of 96 trials on 32 primary test maps. Landmark search is still employed, and it provides a conditional mean paired-map relative score improvement of 0.924% over the case of not using it, while the average search time increases from 0.196 seconds to 0.376 seconds. Comparing the two stage removal methods shows different trade-offs between acceptance, score and computation. The research does not demonstrate a superiority of mean score against PSO-C. The mean paired-map relative enhancement is 0.690%, the median is zero and the descriptive 95% bootstrap interval contains zero. PSO-C allows one additional attempt.
P249 [P5] There are 16 valid motion executions which enable independent diagnosis. All four missions are achieved successfully with both full-scale and half-scale support. Both zero-assistance settings return no completions and timeout at 180 seconds, regardless of whether verified leg-body gravity compensation is present. The established six-foot criterion shows no foot contact for all assisted executions. This research therefore separates the acceptance of a restricted bodily route from the approval of unassisted movement. The mentioned costs and trade-offs are appropriate for the given simulation and objective.
