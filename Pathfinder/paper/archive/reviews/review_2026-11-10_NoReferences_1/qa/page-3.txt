3.2 Route geometry
A path P is a set of points that lie on the horizontal  
plane. The first and last points are the given starting  
and  ending.  Each  point  is  connected  by  a  straight 
segment, in order. The spacing of the first search grid  
is 0.25 m and the directions of the motion are eight.  
Diagonal movement is not allowed at blocked grid  
corners. All returned segments will have to undergo  
the  same  clearance  test  with  the  planar  obstacle  
geometry. The radius of the robot is defined as 1.82 m, 
tracking margin is 0.12 m and the tolerance of segment 
clearance  is  0.005  m.  These  values  are  used  for  
planning  clearance  model.  The  analysis  assumes  a 
trajectory for the robot body and does not generate  
three  dimensional  foot-placement  trajectories.  The  
terrain map is used to specify areas having varying  
walking-speed and difficulty values. Terrain difficulty 
at each grid location is determined by equations (1)  
and (2).

In this case the clip function will only accept the  
first argument from the range [0, 2]. The variables s, h 
and u represent slope (in radians), height range (in  
meters) and  the standard  deviation  of  the  sampled  
ground  heights  (in  meters)  of  the  population,  
respectively, and f represents the semantic softness.  
The heights are measured in nine locations with offsets 
of −1.82, 0 and 1.82 m on each horizontal axis. Slope  
is the mean sampled height (computed on 0.25 m grid) 
gradient  magnitude  (arctangent  of  the  gradient  
magnitude of the mean sampled height). The values of 
f for the various terrain grades tested (grades 0, 1, 2)  
are  0,  0.25,  and  0.60,  respectively.  Terrain-grade  
requests are made with a 1.1 m buffer around patch  
boundaries.  The  difficulty  is  measured  up  to  a  
maximum of every 0.125 m in each segment of a route. 
The difficulty for each route sample is the one of the  
closest  grid  cell.  Segment  exposure  =  length  of  
segment x the mean sampled difficulty. The sum of all 
segments is the total exposure R. This amount is based 
on a terrain map and does not reflect an estimate of slip 
likelihood.

The battery cost model is defined as 0.70 percentage 
points per walking meter and 0.015 per second, 0.08  
per turning radian and a 0.10 start/stop allowance.  
These coefficients have not been adjusted to electrical 
measurements.  The  four  score  terms  are  not  
independent physical outcomes since distance, travel  
time and turning also contribute to E. When they are  
combined they reflect a fixed preference of these route 
properties. The six-point reserve gives an allowance  
and the 1.25 multiplier applies to the outward-and-
return cost. Both rules are not statistically calibrated  
probability of accomplishing a physical mission. The  
same  parameters  are  used  for  all  evaluations.  No  
sensitivity testing is done for different score weights  
or reserve settings. In this paper, battery use refers to  
the output of this equation-based model. The route  
score is not a conversion from mechanical to electrical 
battery work. The charging station and return path are 
predetermined  at  the  start  of  each  trial  prior  to  
comparing planners. In turn, the planners choose an  
outward path to follow, within the same limitation.

experiment,  as  well  as  eight  seconds  of  simulated

times. Whole-mission distance and elapsed time refer
