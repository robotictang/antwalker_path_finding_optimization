4 Planning methods
4.1 Complete planner
CLRP has three stages. The first explores initial  
corridors under time, battery and terrain constraints. A 
corridor is an initial route through free space from  
which later stages generate alternatives. The second  
stage  adds  connections  through  points  around  
obstacles  and  terrain  boundaries.  The  final  stage  
adjusts  routes  by  deleting  internal  points,  moving  
points  toward  their  projections  onto  neighboring  
segments, replacing points with midpoints and moving 
point coordinates.
The best-ranked route is retained throughout the  
search and is replaced only when a candidate improves 
its  rank.  Proposed  routes  must  pass  the  common  
segment-clearance  test  before  their  costs  are  
evaluated.  Repeated  route  geometries  reuse  cached 
evaluations. These rules also apply to the final local-
adjustment stage shared by the evaluated planners.
Table  1  shows  the  time  fractions  of  CLRP,  the 
landmark  cap,  the  size  of  local  moves  and  the  
parameters for initial searches. These settings limit the 
options considered in a trial.
A graph connection and a full route evaluation are  
counted separately. A line segment can be discarded  
by graph search before it is included in a complete  
route.  Graph  search  also  retains  alternatives  with  
different  headings  and  resource  costs.  The  local-
adjustment stage changes a whole sequence of route  
points.  A  common  limit  on  full  route  evaluations  
therefore does not imply equal numbers of geometric  
queries across methods.
Algorithm 1 describes the complete CLRP method. 
Every stage uses the same evaluator and ranking rule.  
Route  evaluations  performed  during  initialization  
count toward the common ceiling.
Algorithm 1. Complete CLRP route search.
Inputs: known obstacle geometry and terrain; start, 
destination and initial heading; task constraints and 
fixed return allowance; random seed; a 2 s search-
time limit, a 240-route-evaluation limit and a shared 
40,000-expansion limit.
1. Run the initial resource-aware refiner for up to 
40% of the nominal search time. Charge its route 
evaluations to the shared limit and retain its best 
available route.
2. Test the direct start-to-destination route if budget 
remains. Reject invalid or blocked geometry. 
Evaluate valid routes using the common metrics 
and constraint-ranking rule; reuse cached 
evaluations for repeated geometry.
3. Collect the initial route’s internal points, inflated 
obstacle corners and points around terrain 
boundaries. Remove duplicate, out-of-bounds and 
blocked points. Sort the remaining candidates by 
the sum of their distances to the start and 
destination. Retain at most 44 points, including the 
start and destination.
4. Build collision-free visibility connections. Search 
the resulting graph with heading-aware states using 
score, time and battery weighting in turn. Limit 
this stage to its 32% nominal-time allocation and at 
most 40,000 minus the expansions used by the 
initial refiner. Evaluate complete routes with the 
common ranking rule.
5. Adjust the best retained route by testing internal-
point deletion, movement halfway toward the 
neighboring-segment projection, midpoint 
replacement and moves in eight directions at step 
sizes of 0.75, 0.25 and 0.08 m. Retain only 
improvements in rank. At each step size, stop after 
three passes, a pass with no improvement, or 
exhaustion of the shared budget.
Output: the best retained route and its metrics, or the 
initial search status if no route is retained. Accepted 
routes always rank ahead of rejected routes.
4.2 PSO and ant controls
PSO-P and ACO-P select subsequences of points  
from the initial corridors and perform the common  
final  local-adjustment  stage.  Table  1  lists  the  
parameters for population sizes, update coefficients  
and iteration limits.
PSO-C  uses  continuous  coordinates  for  internal  
path points. It starts with the same configured initial  
corridor search as CLRP. The longest segments are  
bisected until the path has at least six internal points.  
The subdivision preserves the current corners. Start  
and  end  points  remain  fixed.  The  particle  
representation keeps a fixed number of internal points 
during swarm search; their x and y coordinates may  
change within the map.
